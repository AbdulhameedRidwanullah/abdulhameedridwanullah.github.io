I Don’t Write Like AI

“I am a writer. A writer who also happens to be Kenyan. And I have come to this thesis statement: I don't write like ChatGPT. ChatGPT, in its strange, disembodied, globally-sourced way, writes like me. Or, more accurately, it writes like the millions of us who were pushed through a very particular educational and societal pipeline, a pipeline deliberately designed to sandpaper away ambiguity, and forge our thoughts into a very specific, very formal, and very impressive shape.” - Marcus Olang'.

Marcus Olang has named something many of us felt but haven’t articulated yet. I don’t have much to add but to draw a new reference from another geographic lens. As a Yoruba speaker, my mother's tongue still plays a significant part in how I think and process ideas despite years of training otherwise. So, I can relate to his angle of reasoning. As West Africans, we are products of the same British colonial education system. This system places primacy on refined writing and polished spoken English. .

As late professor Pius Adesanmi once argued, despite his years of working as a professor in Canada, the environment might have only changed his site of enunciation, not the underlying architecture of thought. His Isanlu Isin background and deep connection to Yoruba stay play a part in his intimate layer of thoughts.

The cognitive labour and the effort to sound Oyibo and Polish are often overlooked. Before anything gets written or spoken, it has been processed and filtered, transported from the cultural context to suit the refined standard of training. By the time it gets out, or I put it down on a page, a lot of the messiness has disappeared.

They started with policing “delve”, then moved to the em dash. Some of the things my English teacher, Mr Kuranga would make your behind swell for getting wrong are now casually flagged as AI indicators.

I still remember Mr Sodeeq, who taught me English Literature in Ansarul Islam Secondary School, Ogidi, Ilorin and later Barrister Abdul-Salam Babatunde, insisting that we read newspapers on a daily basis and treat the Advanced Learner’s English Dictionary as a companion.

That was training and indoctrination in reading and writing. This indoctrination builds people who can never afford to be casual in language that was never theirs to begin with. That is why this whole AI-writing discourse feels cruel to many of us. What gets flagged as too clean or too logical is a product of the extra cognitive labour we had to do. But the unfortunate truth is that the overlap is real. The same colonial training that made many of us write in this oyibo language in this form flattened some of the rhythms and textures of how we naturally think. The real question for me is not whether we are misread as AI, but what part of our original voice was buried in the process? Read on LinkedIn
